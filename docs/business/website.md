What the site is for and what it says. How it is built is the website plan's job (`docs/plans/website/`).

## Its job in the funnel (D-57)

Early on, nobody finds the site by accident. Everyone arrives from something the owner did: a call, an email, a
LinkedIn connection, a post. So the site has three jobs, in this order:
1. **Confirm we're real.** In 10 seconds: who we help, what problem, who's behind it.
2. **Show we know the subject.** The weekly blog (D-47), often a piece sent after a call.
3. **Make the next step easy.** Every page points at one action: booking the call.

Later it also brings people in on its own (search on the blog, the brainstorming agent, D-51).

**Audience:** owners of D2C brands that sell in more than one place (D-41, D-52). **The offer on the site is the audit
only** (D-53, D-54). Never the build or the retainer.

## Pages (v1)

| Page | Must have |
|---|---|
| Home | The sections below, in order |
| The audit | What it answers, what they get, how it runs, what we need, how data is kept safe |
| Blog | Index (newest first) and post pages |
| About | A real photo, who I help (D2C brands that sell in more than one place) and how I work, a link to LinkedIn. No day job |
| Book a call | A 30-minute call (D-60). Show the slot times in the visitor's own time zone |
| Privacy policy, contact | Needed once people book calls or share data. A plain contact email |

**Later, not v1:** case studies (after the first clients), the brainstorming agent (D-51), a newsletter (after 8–10
posts).

**Never on the site:** prices, guarantee figures, the retainer or build offers (D-58); client logos, testimonials or
numbers we don't have; stock photos or AI-made images; chat widgets or pop-ups; any real prospect's name or data (D-09).

## Home, top to bottom (draft copy)

1. **Headline, the line under it, and the button**
   > **Your brand runs on multiple systems. The time and money leak between them.**
   >
   > Your store, Amazon, your retailers, your warehouse and your accounts each hold part of the picture. I audit your
   > own data, show you what the gaps are costing, and fix the ones worth fixing.
   >
   > [Book a 30-minute call]
2. **Sound familiar?** These are examples, not limits. Each one maps to an audit type in `offer.md`.
   > - **Numbers that don't add up.** Your site says one thing, Amazon another, and retailer reports arrive in a
   >   different format every time.
   > - **Work that shouldn't be manual.** Purchase orders typed in by hand, stock checked in spreadsheets, a monthly
   >   report someone builds over two days.
   > - **Decisions you can't check.** You added a retailer or launched a product, and you still don't know whether it
   >   paid off.
3. **The audit**
   > Whatever the problem, it starts the same way: one business question, answered from your own data, at a fixed
   > price. You keep the report, whatever happens next.

   Then the 5 things they get (from `offer.md`): what repeats, what it costs, what to fix first, what's ready for AI,
   a 45-minute readout call.
4. **How it works:** a 30-minute call → read-only exports → the readout. One line under each, in the audit page's words:
   "We find the one question worth answering." / "From the systems that hold the answer. Nothing changed." /
   "The report and a 45-minute readout call."
5. **Who's behind it:** one short paragraph and the photo. "AI only where it pays for itself."
6. **From the blog:** the latest 2–3 pieces.
7. **FAQ:**
   - "We already have Klaviyo or a dashboard." → Those show one part well. A decision needs the whole business.
   - "What access do you need, and is our data safe?" → Read-only exports, nothing changed in your systems.
   - "How much does it cost?" → A fixed price, quoted on the call once we know the question.
   - "What if you find nothing worth fixing?" → It's a fixed price, paid upfront, and the report is yours either way. If the gaps are small, it says so, with the numbers.
8. **The button again.**

## Blog requirements

- The owner writes every post (D-44). The site renders them; nobody else writes or edits the words.
- Post pages support: a sources list with links, a label for each kind of number (study, company claim, vendor data,
  estimate, D-45), and a visible "synthetic business" label (D-42).
- Every post ends with the quiet audit line and the booking button.
- Clean reading layout, publish date, an RSS feed, and good link previews for LinkedIn.
- No comments, no share-count widgets.

## Booking

- 30 minutes (D-60), on Cal.com (D-61). Ask only: name, email, company website, where they sell (own site, Amazon, retailers, other), and one
  optional line on what's on their mind.
- A confirmation email with the time in their own time zone.

## Voice and rules

- Plain words; follow `content.md` writing rules and `strategy.md` positioning. Say "I", not "we" (a solo practice).
- **Use "audit", never "diagnostic"** (D-54). Never "AI automation". Opportunity, never fear (D-03).
- No invented numbers anywhere on the site.

## Measuring

- Privacy-friendly analytics with no cookies where possible, so no consent banner is needed. No ad pixels.
- Track: bookings, which page they booked from, and visits to blog posts. Never put personal data in URLs.
