I'm studying how companies make decisions, starting with D2C skincare. Over the last few weeks my research has
covered 637 D2C skincare brands, and there is one pattern I keep coming back to. It isn't about one brand. It shows
up again and again, in small brands and premium ones, in the US, the UK, Canada and Australia. It's about discounts,
and more exactly about who ends up receiving them.

In this piece I want to go slowly. First what I saw, then why I think it matters more than it looks, then a study that
tested this exact question with more than half a million people, and finally the steps to check it in your own store. If you
run a D2C brand, the last part is the one you can use this week.

## What I saw

Of the 637 brands my research covers, 443 offer a discount the moment you sign up. The most common offer is 15%.
Some go to 20% or more.

It makes sense. The first order is the hardest one to win. A stranger has never tried your product, doesn't know if it
will suit their skin, and has a hundred other brands one tab away. A small discount helps them take the risk. Most
founders I know have done the math on this. They treat the signup discount as the price of winning a new customer, and
I think that's a fair call. If it brings in customers who stay, it pays for itself.

The signup discount is not the part I keep thinking about. It's what comes after.

Of the brands that sell a subscription, 278 out of 348 discount the subscription too. That discount is not a one-time
welcome. It applies to every order the subscriber ever places. Many brands run bundles on top of that. Some run a sale
that never really ends; in my research, 129 out of 532 brands had a sale running all year round. And when brands
do run a sale, they often go deep: 208 out of 369 brands had offered 30% or more at some point, and 96 of them 50%
or more.

Put these together and a picture forms. A customer gets 15% on their first order. They subscribe and get another
discount on every refill. They see a bundle that's cheaper again. Then Black Friday comes and the whole store is 30%
off. The discount doesn't stop once the customer is won. It follows them, order after order.

## Why this matters more than it looks

Every one of these discounts is a reasonable decision on its own. The signup code wins new people. The subscription
discount keeps them. The bundle raises the order size. The big sale clears stock and brings a spike in revenue. Each
one has a clear reason, and each one usually shows up as a win on the dashboard.

The question nobody asks is this: when a customer who already loves your product gets a discount on their fifth order,
what is that discount actually buying?

There are two possible answers, and they lead to very different businesses.

The first answer is that it buys loyalty. The customer feels rewarded, buys more often, stays longer, and tells
friends. If that's true, the discount is working.

The second answer is that it buys nothing. The customer was going to reorder anyway, at full price. The discount just
gave away part of the margin on an order you already had. Worse, it may have taught them something: that there is
always a deal if they wait. Next time, they wait.

The tricky part is that both answers look exactly the same on the day of the sale. Orders go up. Revenue goes up.
The dashboard is happy. The difference only shows up months later, in what those customers do next, and almost no
brand follows a sale that far.

To make the first cost concrete, take a made-up brand. It runs a 25% sale for Black Friday, and 2,000 returning
customers buy during it, with an average order of $60. If half of them would have reordered at full price within the
next few weeks anyway, the brand handed those 1,000 customers 1,000 × $60 × 25% = $15,000 of margin for orders it
was already going to get. These numbers are invented to show the arithmetic. Your numbers will be different, and
the whole point of this piece is that you can find them.

That $15,000 is the cost you can see if you look for it. The cost you can't see is the habit. That's what the study
below is about.

## Someone tested exactly this

In 1999, two researchers, Eric Anderson (Kellogg School of Management) and Duncan Simester (MIT Sloan), worked with a
medium-sized US mail-order catalogue company to answer this question properly. The company sold around 450 products
under its own brand, mainly through its own catalogues, to well-educated, older customers. The paper doesn't name
the company and only describes the products as having many of the same features as books or software. They were
durable products, and the average customer bought roughly once every 48 weeks.

What makes this study special is how it was done. They didn't look back at old sales data and guess. They ran
experiments.

The company printed two versions of the same catalogue. Both versions had the same products, the same pages and the
same text. Both showed some items as "Regularly $x, Sale $y". The only difference was how big the sale was. In one
version the sale price was the company's normal kind of discount. In the other version, the same items were much
cheaper. Customers were randomly split into two groups, and each group was mailed one version. After that, both
groups received exactly the same catalogues for the next two years. So any difference in what they bought later could
only come from that one deeper sale.

They ran this three times between January and April 1999 and followed what people bought for the next 22 to 28
months.

- **Study A** was with 56,466 existing customers, people who had bought from the company before. The catalogue had 86
  products, and 36 of them were discounted more deeply in one version. On those 36 items, the regular price averaged
  $203.83. The normal sale price averaged $133.81 and the deep sale price $77.17.
- **Studies B and C** were with people who had never bought from the company. Together, about 540,000 people were
  mailed one of the two versions.

### What happened with new customers

New customers behaved the way any founder would hope.

The deeper sale brought in almost twice as many first orders. In Study B, 0.38% of people who got the deep-sale
catalogue bought, against 0.20% for the normal one. In Study C it was 0.40% against 0.23%.

And the deep sale didn't just win the first order. The new customers it brought in went on to buy more over the
following months. In Study C, they bought 1.33 items each from later catalogues, against 0.99 for customers who came
in through the normal sale. Study B pointed the same way, though the difference there was smaller and less certain.

There was one catch even here. In all three studies, the customers who came in through the deeper sale went on to
choose cheaper items later. They came back more often, but they had already started looking at the low end.

### What happened with existing customers

With existing customers, the picture turned upside down.

On the day, the deep sale looked like a success. 3.19% of existing customers bought from the deep-sale catalogue,
against 2.02% from the normal one, and they bought more items per order (2.14 against 1.59).

Then the researchers followed those same customers for 28 months.

The customers who had bought in the deep sale bought fewer items afterwards (6.89 each against 7.67), and the items
they chose were cheaper ($84.86 on average against $95.51). Put together, their spending over the next 28 months was
$584.68 per customer, against $733.50 for those who had bought in the normal sale. That's about 20% less future
revenue from each customer.

The most telling number is this one. When the researchers added up everything each customer bought, the sale order
plus everything after it, the totals were about the same in both groups (around 9 items each). The deep sale didn't
create more demand from existing customers. It moved demand they already had to an earlier date and a lower price.

### Who it hurt most

When the researchers looked closer, the damage was not spread evenly. It sat with the best customers.

- Customers who had bought **recently and often** were the ones who bought less afterwards.
- Customers who had **usually paid higher prices** in the past shifted the most towards cheaper items. They also
  became more likely to buy only when something was on sale.
- Customers who had bought only once or twice, and **not recently**, behaved like new customers. For them the deeper
  sale increased future buying by about 30%.

In plain words, the deep discount worked on people who didn't know the brand well. On people who already knew it and
valued it, it did something else. It taught them that the price they had been paying was not the real price.

The researchers name the causes in their paper. Some customers stocked up while the price was low and needed less
later. Some became more sensitive to price and started looking for the deal. All of this came from a single deeper
sale, in one catalogue, followed for two years.

## What this means for a D2C skincare brand

A catalogue company in 1999 is not a skincare brand in 2026, and I'll come back to the differences. But the structure
of the problem is the same. You have new customers and existing ones in the same sale. You have one discount being
shown to both. And you have a dashboard that only measures what happens on the day.

If the pattern holds for your brand, it points to a few things worth checking:

1. Discounts aimed at **new and lapsed customers** may be doing their job, and may be worth more than the dashboard
   shows, because those customers keep buying.
2. Discounts that reach your **loyal, recent, full-price customers** may cost you twice: once in margin on orders you
   would have had anyway, and again in the months after, when they wait for the next sale.
3. A **subscription discount** is a special case. It is a permanent discount for the people who are most committed to
   your product. Some of them would have subscribed without it, and some were already buying every month at full
   price before they subscribed.

None of this means discounts are bad. It means the same discount can be a good decision for one group of customers
and an expensive one for another, and most brands can't see the difference because they never split the two.

## How to check this in your own store

You don't need a research team for this. You need your order history and a few hours with a spreadsheet. Here are the
steps I would follow.

**Step 1. Export your orders.** Take the last 24 months from your store. For each order you need: the order date, a
customer ID or email, the order value before discount, the discount amount, the discount code if any, and whether it
was a subscription order. Most store platforms can export this.

**Step 2. Label every order.** Mark each one as the customer's first order or a repeat order, and as full price or
discounted. This simple split is already more than most brands look at.

**Step 3. Group your existing customers the way the study did.** Before your last big sale, put each existing customer
into one of four groups:
- bought recently and often;
- bought often, but not recently;
- bought once or twice, recently;
- bought once or twice, not recently.

Then add one more label: did they mostly pay full price before, or mostly buy on discount?

**Step 4. Look at your last big sale.** Of the discounted orders in that sale, what share came from existing
customers? And how many of those customers had bought at full price in the six months before? That second group is
your "would have bought anyway" group. Multiply their orders by the discount and you have the first cost, the one you
can see.

**Step 5. Follow them for six to twelve months.** For existing customers who bought in the sale, compare what they did
next with similar customers (same group from step 3) who didn't buy in the sale. Look at three things: how many orders
they placed, how long until their next order, and what share of those orders were at full price. If sale buyers drift
towards discounted orders afterwards, that's the habit the study found.

**Step 6. Run a small test of your own.** Step 5 has a weakness. The customers who chose to buy in your sale are not
the same as those who didn't, so the comparison is never perfectly fair. The study avoided this by splitting customers
at random, and you can do the same. Before your next sale, take a random 10 to 20% of your existing customers and send
them a smaller discount, or no sale email at all. Keep everything else the same. Six months later, compare the two
groups. That one test will tell you more about your discounts than any dashboard.

**Step 7. Decide per group, not for the whole store.** Once you know how each group responds, you can treat them
differently. Keep strong offers for new and lapsed customers if they bring people who stay. For your loyal, full-price
customers, test rewards that don't lower the price, such as early access, samples or a gift with the order. I say
test, because that last idea is my suggestion, not something the study proved.

## The limits of this

I want to be clear about what this study does and doesn't show.

- **It is one company.** A medium-sized catalogue firm with older, well-educated customers. Your customers may behave
  differently.
- **The products were durable.** People bought roughly once a year. Skincare runs out and gets reordered far more
  often. That could change the result in either direction. It may make stocking up during a sale even more likely, but
  it also means customers have more chances to drift back to full price.
- **It was 1999 to 2001, by mail.** Today customers see prices across many brands in seconds, and most sales arrive by
  email and social media, not catalogues.
- **It tested one deep sale against a normal sale,** not a sale against no sale. Both groups were offered a discount.
  The study measured what happens when the discount gets deeper. The authors themselves say repeated promotions need
  further study.
- **My numbers on skincare brands describe what brands offer,** not what customers actually use. They come from 637
  brands my research covers, drawn from a company list, not a random sample of every brand in the market. I can see
  the discounts on the website. I can't see anyone's orders.

So I'm not telling you your existing customers are losing 20% of their future spending because of your sales. I'm
telling you that a careful study found that once, that there's no reason to assume your customers are immune, and
that you have the data to check.
